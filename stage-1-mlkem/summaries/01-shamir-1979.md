# How to Share a Secret: Shamir (CACM 1979)

[← back to Stage 1](../README.md) · [Paper](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/359168.359176) · Read 2026-10-18

**Problem.** Split a secret among *n* people so that any *k* of them can recover it, but *k−1* learn
nothing. Done with physical locks, this is a combinatorial explosion: for 11 scientists where any 6
may open the cabinet, it takes 462 locks and 252 keys per scientist.

**Key idea.** Hide the secret as the constant term of a random polynomial of degree *k−1* (mod a
prime *p*), and give each person one point on it. Any *k* points fix the polynomial and therefore the
secret. *k−1* points fit every possible secret equally well, so they reveal nothing: **perfect
secrecy**.

**Result.**
- **Information-theoretic:** security rests on no hardness assumption, so a quantum computer gives
  no advantage.
- **Ideal share size:** each share is the same size as the secret.
- **Hierarchies:** more important parties simply get more shares.

**Limitation.** No cheating detection: a false share goes unnoticed, which later led to verifiable
secret sharing.

**For this thesis.** The first step on the polynomial thread: here a polynomial *hides* a secret; in
Kyber, polynomial rings *build* the encryption. It also sets the contrast for the whole stage:
Shamir's scheme is quantum-safe by construction, while public-key cryptography needs a hard problem.
Kyber picks one that quantum computers are not known to solve.
